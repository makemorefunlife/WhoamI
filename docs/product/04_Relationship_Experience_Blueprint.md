# Part 1. Relationship Experience Constitution

## 1.1 Why This Document Exists

Ahaitsme already produces relationship reports.

The product can calculate deterministic relationship data.

The product can generate AI narratives.

The product can display cards, graphs, summaries, and detailed reports.

However, today's product has grown organically.

Different features were added at different times.

Different relationship products evolved independently.

Different visual components follow different priorities.

As a result, although each feature may work individually, the overall product experience is inconsistent.

This document exists to unify every relationship product under one experience architecture.

It answers one question:

> **How should every relationship report feel, regardless of relationship type?**

This document is not about implementation.

It is about creating a consistent premium experience.

---

## 1.2 Current Reality

Today's system already contains valuable foundations.

Examples include:

- deterministic relationship calculations
- psychological matching
- AI-generated interpretation
- premium reports
- relationship indices
- comparison tables
- graphs
- digest cards
- summaries
- contextual explanations

These systems should not be discarded.

Instead, they should be reorganized into one coherent experience.

This Blueprint therefore focuses on **refinement, not replacement**.

---

## 1.3 Product Goal

The goal is **not** to build a larger report.

The goal is **not** to display more information.

The goal is **not** to impress users with technical depth.

The goal is to help users reach clearer understanding.

Understanding creates confidence.

Confidence supports better decisions.

Ahaitsme never replaces the user's judgment.

It strengthens it.

---

## 1.4 Experience Constitution

Every relationship product must satisfy the following principles.

### 1.4.1 Understanding Before Information

Information alone has little value.

Understanding creates value.

Every section should transform facts into meaning.

Never expose data without explaining why it matters.

---

### 1.4.2 Relationship Before Individuals

The primary subject is never Person A.

The primary subject is never Person B.

The subject is always:

> **The relationship itself.**

Individual traits only matter because they influence the relationship.

---

### 1.4.3 Meaning Before Measurement

Graphs are not the product.

Scores are not the product.

Numbers are not the product.

They exist only to support understanding.

Whenever a conflict exists between:

- showing another metric
- improving understanding

understanding always wins.

---

### 1.4.4 Recognition Before Improvement

Users first want to feel understood.

Advice has no value until recognition has occurred.

The product should consistently create moments where users think:

> "That's exactly us."

before attempting to explain what they should do next.

---

### 1.4.5 Honest Confidence

The system must never pretend certainty where certainty does not exist.

Unknown is better than fabricated precision.

Confidence changes tone.

Confidence never changes facts.

---

### 1.4.6 Human Understanding

The experience should feel like reading the work of an exceptional editor.

Not software.

Not an expert system.

Not a psychology textbook.

Not fortune telling.

---

### 1.4.7 One Product Family, Distinct Products

Although Romantic, Marriage / Cohabitation, Friend, Work, and Family (Child / Parent perspectives) answer different life questions,

they should always feel like one product family.

Users should immediately recognize:

> "This was made by Ahaitsme."

Shared experience principles do **not** mean interchangeable content.

Domain differences are product differences—questions, stakes, modules, and emphasis—not only copy or terminology swaps.

---

## 1.5 What We Are Designing

This Blueprint does not design reports.

It designs experiences.

The report is only one part of the experience.

The complete experience includes:

- entering with questions
- discovering insights
- reflecting
- making decisions
- sharing
- returning later

Every interaction contributes to the product experience.

---

## 1.6 Product Experience Model

Every relationship product inherits the same **high-level journey stages** as a shared vocabulary.

```text
Question
↓
Understanding
↓
Recognition
↓
Discovery
↓
Reflection
↓
Decision Support
↓
Action
↓
Return
```

Shared stages do **not** mean every product uses the same report modules, the same depth, or the same decision questions.

Domain Blueprints choose which stages to emphasize, which modules to include, and which life domains matter.

---

## 1.7 Design Philosophy

Relationship reports are not reference documents.

They are reading experiences.

Users should never feel they are reading generated content.

Instead, they should feel that someone has carefully organized everything they need to understand.

Good experiences reduce effort.

They do not increase effort.

Premium quality is measured by how naturally users progress through understanding, not by report length.

## 1.8 Inheritance

Every relationship product inherits this Blueprint.

Relationship-specific documents define what **must** differ by product: life questions, modules, emphasis, stakes, and perspective.

Everything else—shared principles below—remains common.

```text
Shared Experience Blueprint (this document)
↓
Romantic Blueprint
Marriage / Cohabitation Blueprint
Friend Blueprint
Work Blueprint
Family — Child Perspective Blueprint
Family — Parent Perspective Blueprint
```

This prevents duplicated philosophy while keeping products distinct where life context differs.

Romantic must not absorb Marriage / Cohabitation topics (household, money, in-laws, parenting, long-term residence, etc.).

Marriage / Cohabitation must not be reduced to Romantic attraction / early-relationship framing.

Family Child and Family Parent are separate perspective products under the Family family.

## 1.9 Implementation Philosophy

This Blueprint is implementation-aware.

It is not theoretical.

Every principle should eventually map to:

deterministic logic
UX components
information architecture
LLM behavior
interaction design
visual hierarchy

If a principle cannot realistically be implemented,

it should not exist in this document.

This Blueprint serves as the bridge between philosophy and production.

# Part 2. Relationship Experience Journey

## 2.1 Why Journey Matters

Relationship reports are often treated as static documents.

Ahaitsme is not building a document.

Ahaitsme is building a guided thinking experience.

The user's understanding should evolve while reading.

Each section should naturally prepare the user for the next.

The report is therefore designed as a journey rather than a collection of independent pages.

---

## 2.2 The Relationship Journey

Every relationship report follows the same experience journey.


Curiosity
↓
Orientation
↓
Recognition
↓
Understanding
↓
Discovery
↓
Reflection
↓
Decision Support
↓
Confidence
↓
Action


Each stage has a specific purpose.

Skipping stages weakens trust.

Changing the order reduces emotional impact.

---

## 2.3 Stage 1 — Curiosity

### 2.3.1 User State

The user enters with uncertainty.

Examples:

- "Why do we fight?"
- "Why are we so comfortable together?"
- "Are we compatible?"
- "Is this relationship healthy?"
- "How are we different?"

The user is not looking for data.

The user is looking for answers.

---

### 2.3.2 Product Goal

Reduce uncertainty.

Create curiosity.

Promise understanding.

Not conclusions.

---

### 2.3.3 Design Principles

The first screen should answer:

> "This report understands what you're trying to figure out."

It should never begin with:

- raw scores
- long explanations
- psychological terminology
- technical calculations

Instead,

the product should immediately establish context.

## 2.4 Stage 2 — Orientation

Once curiosity is established,

the user needs orientation.

They should understand:

- who this report is about
- what questions it answers
- what they are about to read
- how to read it

Orientation reduces cognitive load.

Users should never wonder:

"What am I looking at?"

---

### 2.4.1 Product Goal

Build confidence.

Provide structure.

Reduce confusion.

---

### 2.4.2 Examples

Good orientation:

"We analyzed how the two of you naturally interact."

Bad orientation:

"Compatibility Score: 78"

A score without context has little meaning.

---

## 2.5 Stage 3 — Recognition

Recognition is the emotional foundation of trust.

Users should frequently think:

> "That's exactly us."

Recognition creates permission.

Only after users feel understood are they willing to accept deeper interpretation.

---

### 2.5.1 Recognition Sources

Recognition may come from:

- relationship dynamics

- emotional patterns

- communication styles

- recurring conflicts

- strengths

- habits

- priorities

Recognition should happen repeatedly throughout the report.

Not only once.

---

### 2.5.2 Design Rule

Recognition must occur before improvement.

Never attempt to "fix" a relationship before demonstrating understanding.

---

## 2.6 Stage 4 — Understanding

Recognition answers:

"That feels true."

Understanding answers:

"Now I understand why."

This is where deterministic analysis provides meaning.

The product connects:

Facts

↓

Patterns

↓

Meaning

↓

Relationship impact

Every explanation should help users connect events that previously felt unrelated.

---

### 2.6.1 Product Goal

Transform isolated observations into a coherent mental model.

The report should simplify complexity.

Not increase it.

## 2.7 Stage 5 — Discovery

Once users understand existing patterns,

they become ready for new insights.

Discovery should feel surprising,

but inevitable.

Examples:

"I never realized..."

"So that's why..."

"That explains..."

These moments create the strongest emotional value.

---

### 2.7.1 Discovery Rules

Discovery must always emerge from previous evidence.

It should never appear random.

Unexpected does not mean unsupported.

---

## 2.8 Stage 6 — Reflection

Reflection slows the experience.

Users stop reading.

They begin thinking.

Reflection is where the report becomes personally meaningful.

Without reflection,

reports become entertainment.

---

### 2.8.1 Reflection Questions

The product should naturally encourage thoughts like:

- Is this true?

- Have we experienced this?

- When did this happen?

- How often does this happen?

Reflection is often more valuable than explanation.

---

## 2.9 Stage 7 — Decision Support

This is not advice.

This is structured support.

Ahaitsme never decides.

Users decide.

The product helps organize information so better decisions become easier.

---

### 2.9.1 Decision Support Examples

Instead of

"You should break up."

The product says

"These recurring patterns may become more difficult if neither side changes."

Instead of

"You should marry."

The product says

"The current relationship demonstrates long-term stability across multiple areas."

The distinction is critical.

## 2.10 Stage 8 — Confidence

Confidence is the result of the journey.

It is not a score.

It is a feeling.

Users should finish thinking:

"I understand this relationship much better."

Not

"The AI predicted our future."

---

### 2.10.1 Success Indicator

Confidence increases when:

- users understand disagreements

- users understand strengths

- users understand differences

- users understand themselves

Confidence comes from clarity.

Never certainty.

---

## 2.11 Stage 9 — Action

The report ends with possibility.

Not finality.

Users should naturally ask:

"What do we do next?"

Depending on product,

action may include:

- better conversations

- healthier expectations

- clearer boundaries

- appreciation

- continued reflection

The report should inspire healthier behavior,

not dependence on the product.

---

## 2.12 Journey Integrity

Each stage depends on the previous stage.

Curiosity

↓

Orientation

↓

Recognition

↓

Understanding

↓

Discovery

↓

Reflection

↓

Decision Support

↓

Confidence

↓

Action

Skipping a stage without a documented product reason weakens the experience.

Domain Blueprints may compress or de-emphasize a stage when it does not apply—but must not invent a contradictory journey that abandons recognition before advice, progressive disclosure, or honest confidence.

The journey should feel invisible.

Users should never notice the structure.

They should simply feel that the report "flows naturally."

---

## 2.13 Journey Ownership

This experience journey is shared as a **constitution** across:

- Romantic
- Marriage / Cohabitation
- Friend
- Work
- Family — Child Perspective
- Family — Parent Perspective

Relationship-specific Blueprints may customize:

- which stages are primary versus brief
- examples and wording
- emphasis and decision questions
- which Core Dynamic Modules appear
- which life domains are in-scope

They must **not**:

- replace the shared reading hierarchy (Result → Evidence → Meaning → Action)
- treat domain differences as copy-only substitutions under one identical module set
- merge Romantic with Marriage / Cohabitation
- collapse Family Child and Family Parent into one undifferentiated report

Consistency is part of the Ahaitsme identity.

Distinct products remain distinct.

---
# Part 3. Information Architecture

## 3.1 Why Information Architecture Matters

A relationship report is not simply a collection of insights.

It is a carefully structured conversation.

Every piece of information changes how the next piece is interpreted.

The order therefore matters as much as the content itself.

Users should never feel that information is randomly assembled.

Instead, every section should answer the next natural question that appears in the reader's mind.

Good Information Architecture reduces thinking effort.

Poor Information Architecture forces users to organize the report themselves.

Ahaitsme should always do that work for the user.

---

## 3.2 Information Hierarchy

Every relationship report follows the same hierarchy.

```

Big Picture

↓

Key Relationship Signals

↓

Core Dynamics

↓

Evidence

↓

Detailed Exploration

↓

Reflection

↓

Decision Support

```

The amount of information increases,

while cognitive effort decreases.

The user should never feel overwhelmed.

---

## 3.3 Layered Information Model

Information is revealed progressively.

Users should never receive every detail immediately.

Instead, every layer answers one level of curiosity before introducing the next.

```

Layer 1

Can I understand this relationship quickly?

↓

Layer 2

Why is it like this?

↓

Layer 3

What evidence supports that?

↓

Layer 4

What does this mean long-term?

↓

Layer 5

How should I think about this?

```

Every report should follow this progression.

---

## 3.4 Layer 1 — Instant Understanding

This is the first meaningful experience.

Its job is not explanation.

Its job is orientation.

Within a few seconds,

users should already understand:

- the overall relationship feeling
- the biggest strengths
- the biggest challenges
- where attention should go next

Everything on this screen should answer:

"What kind of relationship is this?"

Not:

"How was this calculated?"

---

### 3.4.1 Components

Typical components include:

- Hero Summary
- Relationship Snapshot
- Key Relationship Cards
- Primary Indicators

No detailed evidence should appear yet.

## 3.5 Layer 2 — Core Dynamics

Once users understand the relationship at a high level,

they naturally ask:

"Why?"

This layer answers that question.

Instead of discussing isolated traits,

the report explains interaction.

Examples:

- emotional rhythm
- conflict pattern
- communication style
- decision pattern
- balance of influence
- energy exchange

This becomes the conceptual model of the relationship.

---

### 3.5.1 Components

Examples include:

- Core Dynamic Cards

- Relationship Balance

- Communication Pattern

- Emotional Pattern

- Conflict Pattern

- Shared Strengths

Each component explains interaction,

not personality.

---

## 3.6 Layer 3 — Supporting Evidence

Every important statement should have evidence.

Evidence may come from:

- deterministic calculations

- relationship rules

- psychological dimensions

- repeated signals

- multiple independent indicators

Evidence increases credibility.

It should never interrupt reading flow.

Evidence supports understanding.

It should never dominate the experience.

---

### 3.6.1 Evidence Philosophy

Evidence exists for trust,

not curiosity.

Most users care less about calculations

than about whether conclusions feel justified.

---

## 3.7 Layer 4 — Deep Exploration

This layer satisfies highly curious users.

Not everyone will read it.

That is acceptable.

Deep Exploration includes:

- detailed interaction analysis

- individual dimensions

- psychological interpretation

- relationship context

- nuanced explanations

This layer should reward curiosity,

not punish it.

Readers who continue should feel that every section adds meaningful depth.

## 3.8 Layer 5 — Reflection

The report gradually transitions

from explanation

to interpretation.

Reflection is intentionally slower.

Users should pause.

Think.

Reconnect the report with real experiences.

The report is no longer describing the relationship.

It is helping users interpret their own lives.

---

## 3.9 Information Priority

Not every insight deserves equal visibility.

Importance determines placement.

The report should prioritize information using four levels.

```

Critical

↓

Important

↓

Supporting

↓

Reference

```

---

### 3.9.1 Critical Information

Appears immediately.

Examples:

- defining relationship characteristic

- strongest relationship strength

- greatest recurring challenge

- major compatibility insight

Critical information shapes the entire reading experience.

---

### 3.9.2 Important Information

Supports understanding.

Examples:

- emotional tendencies

- communication rhythm

- shared values

- conflict style

These sections deepen the story.

---

### 3.9.3 Supporting Information

Useful,

but not essential.

Examples:

- secondary dynamics

- contextual explanations

- personality nuance

Supporting information should never interrupt the main narrative.

---

### 3.9.4 Reference Information

Reference information answers:

"I want to know more."

Examples:

- detailed calculations

- methodology

- confidence explanation

- terminology

Reference material should remain available,

but never compete with the story.

---

## 3.10 Progressive Disclosure

Information should unfold naturally.

Never reveal everything at once.

Good progression creates momentum.

```

Question

↓

Answer

↓

Evidence

↓

Meaning

↓

Reflection

````

Each answer should naturally create the next question.

## 3.11 Component Hierarchy

Every visual component exists for a different purpose.

No component should duplicate another.

---

### 3.11.1 Numbers

Purpose:

Measurement.

Numbers answer:

"How much?"

Examples:

- indices

- alignment scores

- confidence

Numbers should never attempt to tell stories.

---

### 3.11.2 Graphs

Purpose:

Pattern recognition.

Graphs answer:

"What shape does this relationship have?"

Examples:

- radar chart

- comparison profile

- emotional balance

Graphs reveal relationships between dimensions.

They should never replace explanation.

---

### 3.11.3 Cards

Purpose:

Immediate understanding.

Cards answer:

"What should I notice first?"

Cards summarize.

They do not teach.

Every card should communicate one idea only.

If a card requires paragraphs,

it should not be a card.

---

### 3.11.4 Narrative

Purpose:

Meaning.

Narrative answers:

"Why does this happen?"

Narrative connects information.

It explains.

It synthesizes.

It provides emotional understanding.

Narrative should never repeat

what cards or graphs already communicate.

---

### 3.11.5 Tables

Purpose:

Comparison.

Tables answer:

"Where are the differences?"

Good tables reduce scanning effort.

Bad tables duplicate paragraphs.

Only use tables when comparison is the primary task.

---

## 3.12 Information Ownership

Every insight should appear once.

If information appears twice,

one of the two should change purpose.

Example:

Relationship Index

↓

Measures relationship health.

Narrative

↓

Explains relationship health.

The same information should never be repeated using different wording.

---

## 3.13 Digest vs Deep Report

Digest and Deep Report are not shorter and longer versions of the same report.

They answer different questions.

---

### 3.13.1 Digest

Purpose:

Immediate understanding.

Questions answered:

- What kind of relationship is this?

- What stands out?

- What deserves attention?

Reading time:

Approximately 2–5 minutes.

---

### 3.13.2 Deep Report

Purpose:

Comprehensive understanding.

Questions answered:

- Why?

- How?

- What patterns continue?

- What creates these outcomes?

Reading time:

15–30 minutes.

Deep Reports expand understanding.

They do not simply add more paragraphs.

---

## 3.14 Information Density

Premium products do not maximize information.

They maximize understanding per minute.

The ideal report feels surprisingly short,

despite containing significant depth.

Every paragraph must justify its existence.

Every component must reduce cognitive effort.

---

## 3.15 Blueprint Implications

This Information Architecture directly determines:

- page order

- UI hierarchy

- report generation

- LLM responsibilities

- deterministic calculation exposure

- graph placement

- card placement

- future product scalability

No implementation should violate this architecture without a documented reason.

# Part 4. Experience Component System

## Purpose

This chapter defines how Ahaitsme converts relationship analysis into a coherent product experience.

It governs the use of:

- Hero summaries
- Relationship indices
- Key signal cards
- Charts and graphs
- Comparison views
- Axis-level data
- Evidence
- Narrative
- Reflection prompts
- Action guidance

The goal is not to maximize the number of components.

The goal is to assign each component one clear responsibility and arrange them so that the user understands the relationship with minimal effort.

This chapter applies to every relationship product.

Relationship-specific Blueprints may define different content, but they must preserve the component responsibilities established here.

---

# 4.1 Current Product Reality

Ahaitsme is not starting from zero.

The current product already contains working output across several layers:

- deterministic relationship calculations
- Saju-based relationship signals
- psychological dimensions
- shared and individual axis data
- relationship indices
- digest cards
- comparison tables
- charts
- premium sections
- AI-generated summaries and narratives
- relationship-specific analysis pipelines

The problem is not the absence of output.

The problem is that the output was developed at different times and under different assumptions.

This has created several recurring issues:

- classification and explanation are sometimes mixed
- cards, charts, and prose may repeat the same conclusion
- relationship-level insights and individual traits may compete for attention
- some numbers appear more precise than the underlying evidence supports
- axis data may be exposed without a clear user question
- important insights may be buried inside long narrative sections
- minor insights may receive the same visual weight as major ones
- LLM prose may compensate for weak information architecture
- similar components may behave differently across relationship products
- visual polish may exist locally without forming one coherent report

The target is therefore not a ground-up rebuild.

The target is a controlled reorganization of existing assets into a shared experience system.

Existing calculations should be preserved when valid.

Existing components should be reused when they serve a clear role.

Existing output should be removed, merged, or demoted when it duplicates another component or weakens the reading journey.

---

# 4.2 Component Constitution

Every component must satisfy all five conditions below.

## 1. It answers a specific user question

A component must have one primary question.

Examples:

| Component | Primary question |
|---|---|
| Hero Summary | What kind of relationship is this? |
| Key Signal Card | What should I notice first? |
| Relationship Index | Where is this relationship relatively strong or strained? |
| Comparison Graph | How are the two people different? |
| Narrative | Why does this pattern occur between them? |
| Evidence Drawer | What supports this interpretation? |
| Reflection Prompt | Where does this appear in real life? |
| Action Guidance | What can we do with this understanding? |

A component without a clear question should not exist.

---

## 2. It has one dominant responsibility

Components may support one another.

They must not perform the same job.

A score should measure.

A graph should reveal shape or contrast.

A card should prioritize.

Narrative should explain meaning.

Evidence should justify.

Guidance should support action.

When one component attempts to do all of these, the experience becomes dense and repetitive.

---

## 3. It earns its visual weight

Visual prominence must reflect insight importance.

A large component communicates:

> This matters.

The product must never give a secondary observation the same weight as a defining relationship pattern.

Component size, order, contrast, and spacing are part of the meaning system.

---

## 4. It is supported by sufficient evidence

No component should become prominent merely because content is available.

Prominence depends on:

- relevance to the relationship
- strength of evidence
- confidence
- distinctiveness
- likely user value

Weak or ambiguous signals may appear as supporting context.

They should not become headline conclusions.

---

## 5. It does not duplicate another component

Every visible insight should have one canonical home.

Other components may reference it, but must add a different function.

Example:

- Card: identifies a recovery pattern
- Graph: shows the difference that contributes to it
- Narrative: explains how the pattern unfolds
- Guidance: suggests what helps the pair recover

This is progressive understanding.

Repeating the same sentence four times is not.

---

# 4.3 The Shared Experience Stack

Every relationship report should be assembled from the following shared stack.

```text
1. Identity and Context
2. Hero Interpretation
3. Key Relationship Signals
4. Relationship Measurement
5. Relationship Shape
6. Core Dynamic Modules
7. Supporting Evidence
8. Reflection
9. Decision Support
10. Action and Return
````

Not every product must expose every layer with equal depth.

However, the order should remain stable unless a relationship-specific Blueprint documents a strong reason to change it.

---

# 4.4 Layer 1 — Identity and Context

## Purpose

Confirm who and what the report is about.

This layer establishes trust before interpretation begins.

## Required information

- names or clear relationship labels
- relationship type
- relevant context, when available
- report scope
- analysis status or date when useful

## Design rule

This layer should feel personal but lightweight.

It should not resemble a technical input summary.

## Avoid

- raw birth data as the visual focus
- survey metadata
- engine version information
- methodology details
- long disclaimers

Technical and methodological information belongs in reference areas.

---

# 4.5 Layer 2 — Hero Interpretation

## Purpose

Give the user an immediate, relationship-level understanding.

The Hero answers:

> What is the defining shape of this relationship?

It is the opening interpretation, not an index dashboard.

## Required content

A complete Hero should normally include:

1. a relationship-level headline
2. a concise interpretive summary
3. one defining strength
4. one central tension or growth edge
5. a clear transition into the report

## Hero headline

The headline should describe the relationship dynamic.

Good:

- Steady on the outside, emotionally faster underneath
- Different rhythms, strong mutual grounding
- Easy connection with a hidden decision gap

Weak:

- Compatibility Report
- Relationship Analysis
- 82% Compatible
- Person A and Person B

## Hero summary

The summary should synthesize multiple relevant signals.

It should not be a rewritten score label.

It should communicate:

- the relationship's defining pattern
- why the pattern matters
- how strength and tension coexist

## Hero restrictions

The Hero must not:

- predict the future
- declare relationship success or failure
- diagnose either person
- expose technical evidence
- contain more than one primary narrative
- begin with a percentage
- flatten the relationship into a single verdict

---

# 4.6 Layer 3 — Key Relationship Signals

## Purpose

Identify the small number of patterns the user should notice first.

These are not generic categories.

They are the most decision-relevant and experientially recognizable signals in this specific relationship.

---

## The Three-Card Default

The default Digest should present **three key relationship cards**.

Three is the preferred default because it creates:

- enough breadth to avoid oversimplification
- enough restraint to preserve prioritization
- a readable first-screen rhythm
- a clear bridge between summary and deeper analysis

However, the number three is a presentation default, not a truth requirement.

The system must not fabricate or promote weak signals merely to fill three spaces.

Allowed behavior:

- 3 cards when three strong distinct signals exist
- 2 cards when only two signals meet the threshold
- 1 card in limited-data or low-confidence cases
- 4 cards only when the relationship-specific Blueprint documents a justified exception

The system must never display empty, generic, or redundant cards to preserve layout symmetry.

---

## Card Selection Principles

Cards should be selected using four filters.

### 1. Relationship relevance

Does the signal describe how these two people interact?

Individual traits without interaction value should not become key cards.

### 2. User value

Would recognizing this pattern materially improve understanding?

A technically interesting signal may still have low product value.

### 3. Evidence strength

Is the pattern supported by one strong source or multiple aligned sources?

Low-confidence signals should not lead the experience.

### 4. Distinctiveness

Does the card add a new idea?

Three differently worded versions of communication difficulty are still one insight.

---

## Required Card Portfolio

The three-card set should normally create a balanced portfolio.

Preferred structure:

```text
Card 1 — Defining relationship dynamic
Card 2 — Strongest stabilizing resource
Card 3 — Most meaningful tension or growth edge
```

This is not a rigid positive-positive-negative formula.

The actual portfolio should reflect the relationship.

The purpose is balance, not forced optimism.

Possible portfolios include:

```text
Dynamic + Strength + Tension
Dynamic + Difference + Recovery Resource
Strength + Hidden Friction + Practical Guidance
Connection Pattern + Social Rhythm + Conflict Pattern
```

---

## Card Anatomy

Each card should include:

- short label
- insight headline
- concise explanation
- confidence-aware tone
- optional directional cue
- optional deep-link to the relevant module

A card should normally communicate one idea in approximately 40–90 words.

If the insight requires multiple paragraphs, it belongs in a module.

---

## Card Content Rules

A card must:

- describe the relationship rather than one person alone
- include meaning, not only classification
- use plain human language
- remain understandable without methodology knowledge
- avoid repeating the Hero summary
- connect to a deeper section when available

A card must not:

- present unsupported certainty
- include multiple unrelated insights
- use a score as its headline
- become a miniature report section
- repeat a chart caption
- expose internal enum names
- include generic advice that could apply to anyone

---

## Candidate Signal Families

The shared system may select from signal families such as:

- connection and emotional accessibility
- communication rhythm
- expression speed
- reassurance pattern
- conflict activation
- recovery speed
- residual tension
- balance of influence
- decision alignment
- practical coordination
- social energy and social exposure
- autonomy and closeness
- recognition and appreciation
- unconscious role distribution
- adaptability under change
- stability and long-term maintenance

Relationship-specific Blueprints determine which families are available and how they are interpreted.

The shared system defines only how selected signals are presented.

# 4.7 Social Energy Must Be Treated as a Relationship Dynamic

Social behavior is not a minor personality footnote.

Differences in social appetite can affect:

- schedule
- rest
- boundaries
- friendships
- jealousy
- public behavior
- family obligations
- hosting
- travel
- lifestyle design
- perceived affection
- emotional recovery

A highly social person and a socially selective person may experience the same weekend very differently.

The product must distinguish between:

- social energy
- sociability
- stimulation tolerance
- need for recovery
- preference for group versus one-to-one connection
- relationship openness to outside people
- pressure to participate
- cultural expectations around social life

The product must not reduce this to:

- extrovert versus introvert
- active versus passive
- social versus antisocial

When evidence is strong and the difference is meaningful, social rhythm may become:

- a key card
- a graph dimension
- a dedicated module
- a practical coordination insight

This consideration is especially important for products serving users in the United States and other highly pluralistic contexts, where romantic and family relationships often interact with:

- independent friendship networks
- work networking
- blended families
- community participation
- cultural differences
- different expectations regarding personal space and couple time

The system should describe the practical relationship impact rather than impose one culturally preferred lifestyle.

# 4.8 Layer 4 — Relationship Measurement

## Purpose

Measurement helps users locate relative strength, strain, alignment, or difference.

Measurement must clarify the relationship.

It must not create false scientific authority.

---

## Measurement Categories

All visible measurements must belong to one of four categories.

### A. Directly calculated measurement

Derived from explicit deterministic inputs and rules.

Examples:

- axis values
- measured differences
- normalized alignment
- repeated signal counts

### B. Composite index

Combines multiple defined inputs according to a documented formula.

Examples:

- relational stability index
- recovery capacity index
- decision coordination index

### C. Categorical classification

Represents a type, range, or state rather than a continuous quantity.

Examples:

- aligned
- complementary
- mixed
- strained
- insufficient evidence

### D. Confidence indicator

Communicates how strongly the system can support an interpretation.

Confidence must not be confused with relationship quality.

---

## Relationship Index Policy

A Relationship Index may be used only when:

1. the construct is clearly defined
2. the inputs are documented
3. the weighting is stable
4. the output can be explained
5. the number adds value beyond a category
6. the interface avoids treating it as destiny

A single total compatibility percentage is not the default product center.

Relationships are multidimensional.

One number may hide whether the apparent result comes from:

- emotional connection
- practical coordination
- similar habits
- complementary differences
- conflict recovery
- long-term values
- social rhythm
- decision style

When a total index is retained, it must be presented as an orientation tool rather than a verdict.

It must be accompanied by meaningful subdimensions.

---

## Recommended Measurement Structure

Preferred:

```text
Relationship Overview
├── Connection
├── Coordination
├── Tension Management
└── Sustainability
```

Relationship-specific products may rename or refine these dimensions.

The structure should remain limited enough to understand quickly.

Five to seven top-level indices should be treated as an upper bound for a summary view.

---

## Score Display Rules

Every displayed score must provide:

- a clear label
- an understandable construct
- a visible range or reference
- a short interpretation
- access to methodology or evidence
- consistent directionality

Users should know whether a higher number means:

- more strength
- more similarity
- more intensity
- more risk
- more confidence

These meanings must never be mixed.

---

## False Precision

Do not display precision beyond what the system can defend.

Avoid:

- 78.43%
- arbitrary decimals
- rankings without a valid comparison population
- exact predictions
- high-resolution gauges based on sparse inputs

Preferred:

- rounded values
- ranges
- categorical bands
- directional indicators
- uncertainty states

---

## Low-Confidence State

When evidence is insufficient, display:

- insufficient evidence
- tentative signal
- not enough information
- mixed indicators

Do not silently convert missing evidence into an average score.

An average-looking result can falsely imply normality or neutrality.

Unknown must remain unknown.

---

# 4.9 Layer 5 — Relationship Shape

## Purpose

Graphs help users see patterns that are difficult to understand in prose.

A graph should answer:

> What relationship pattern becomes visible when these dimensions are seen together?

Graphs are not decoration.

They should be removed when they do not improve comprehension.

---

# 4.10 Chart Selection Framework

Use the chart that matches the user question.

| User question                                     | Preferred visual                   |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Where are the two people different?               | Paired comparison bars or dot plot |
| What is the overall relationship profile?         | Limited-dimension profile chart    |
| Which areas are strongest or weakest?             | Ranked horizontal bars             |
| How balanced is an interaction?                   | Diverging scale or balance bar     |
| How does a pattern unfold over time?              | Sequence or cycle diagram          |
| How do several factors contribute to one outcome? | Contribution view                  |
| What evidence supports a conclusion?              | Signal stack or source map         |
| How confident is the interpretation?              | Confidence band or simple label    |

---

## Radar Chart Policy

Radar charts may be visually attractive but are often difficult to compare accurately.

They should be used only when:

- the number of dimensions is limited
- every axis uses the same scale
- the dimensions form a meaningful profile
- the goal is holistic shape recognition
- exact comparison is not the primary task

Radar charts should not be the default method for comparing two people across all 11 axes.

When precise differences matter, paired bars or dot plots are usually clearer.

---

## Comparison Graph Policy

A person-to-person comparison graph should show:

- both values
- the size of the gap
- the direction of the gap
- the relationship meaning of the gap

The graph alone should not label a gap as good or bad.

Difference can represent:

- complementarity
- friction
- specialization
- learning opportunity
- contextual risk
- no meaningful effect

Interpretation belongs in the accompanying explanation.

---

## Graph Annotation

Every graph should have:

1. a plain-language title
2. one sentence explaining what to notice
3. clear labels
4. accessible scale direction
5. optional expandable interpretation

Bad:

> Psychological Axis Comparison

Better:

> You recharge differently after social and emotional intensity.

---

## Graph Density

A graph should normally display:

- 3–7 dimensions for immediate comprehension
- up to 11 dimensions only in a dedicated detailed view

The summary experience should never expose all available axes merely because they exist.

---

# 4.11 The Role of the 11 Psychological Axes

The 11-axis system is an analysis foundation.

It is not automatically a user-facing navigation system.

The axes are:

- stimulation
- self_control
- practicality
- structure
- empathy
- conflict_style
- resilience
- recognition
- energy_style
- thinking_style
- decision_style

These axes may help explain many relationship patterns.

However, users do not primarily arrive asking:

> What is our score on self_control?

They arrive asking:

- Why do we make decisions differently?
- Why does one of us need more reassurance?
- Why do social plans exhaust one person?
- Why do arguments linger?
- Why does one person want structure while the other adapts?

The experience must translate axes into relationship questions.

---

## Axis Usage Model

The 11 axes may serve four roles.

### 1. Evidence

Axis values support relationship interpretations.

### 2. Comparison

A graph may show meaningful individual differences.

### 3. Explanation

Narrative may connect multiple axes to one interaction pattern.

### 4. Deep reference

Advanced users may explore the full axis profile.

The axes should not automatically become 11 equal report sections.

---

## Axis Promotion Rules

An axis should become prominent only when it is:

- materially different between the two people
- unusually strong or weak for both
- directly relevant to a major relationship pattern
- supported by contextual evidence
- useful to a current user question

An axis should remain in the background when it adds little meaning.

---

## Cross-Axis Synthesis

The strongest insights often emerge from combinations.

Examples:

```text
energy_style + stimulation
→ social rhythm and recovery needs
```

```text
empathy + conflict_style + resilience
→ emotional repair pattern
```

```text
structure + practicality + self_control
→ household and execution coordination
```

```text
thinking_style + decision_style
→ how the pair reaches conclusions
```

```text
recognition + empathy
→ how appreciation is noticed and received
```

The system should prefer meaningful synthesis over isolated axis commentary.

---

## Axis Ownership

Deterministic systems own:

- axis values
- difference calculations
- thresholds
- classifications
- confidence
- eligible interpretations

Narrative systems may:

- explain the relationship meaning
- connect aligned evidence
- provide readable examples
- adapt tone and phrasing

Narrative systems must not:

- invent axis values
- reverse directional findings
- create unsupported classifications
- promote an ineligible axis
- imply clinical diagnosis

---

# 4.12 Layer 6 — Core Dynamic Modules

## Purpose

Core Dynamic Modules provide the main body of the report.

Each module answers one meaningful relationship question.

Modules should be organized around life experience, not engine structure.

---

## Shared Module Anatomy

Every Core Dynamic Module should follow a common sequence.

```text
1. Question
2. Relationship finding
3. Recognizable pattern
4. Why it happens
5. Supporting evidence
6. Situational variation
7. Reflection
8. Practical implication
```

Not every module requires all eight elements visibly expanded.

The structure defines the content logic.

---

## Module Opening

A module should begin with its conclusion.

The user should not read several paragraphs before learning the point.

Recommended:

> You tend to recover at different speeds after conflict.

Then explain:

- who moves toward resolution first
- what the other person may still be processing
- how the mismatch can be misread
- what evidence supports the conclusion

---

## Module Scope

One module should address one dynamic family.

Illustrative catalog across products (not a mandatory set for every domain):

- Emotional Connection
- Communication Rhythm
- Conflict and Recovery
- Decision-Making
- Social Energy
- Practical Coordination
- Recognition and Reassurance
- Autonomy and Closeness
- Adaptation Under Stress

Relationship-specific Blueprints define which modules are appropriate.

Romantic modules center attraction, affection, emotional compatibility, misunderstanding, conflict/repair, and relationship development—not household operation.

Marriage / Cohabitation modules may center money, labor, responsibility, in-law boundaries, personal space, intimacy, children/parenting, values, and long-term household life—without collapsing into Romantic framing.

Family products select modules for Child Perspective or Parent Perspective explicitly.

---

## Module Length

Module length should follow insight complexity.

Do not force equal word counts.

A well-supported complex pattern may require more explanation.

A weak or simple pattern should remain brief.

Visual consistency should not create editorial padding.

---

# 4.13 Layer 7 — Supporting Evidence

## Purpose

Evidence establishes trust without interrupting the primary reading journey.

Evidence should be accessible in layers.

---

## Evidence Levels

### Level 1 — Plain-language support

Visible within the main narrative.

Example:

> Both analyses point to a strong need for structure, but your decision speeds differ.

### Level 2 — Signal summary

Expandable evidence list.

Example:

- shared high structure
- decision-style gap
- repeated planning alignment
- mixed adaptability signal

### Level 3 — Technical reference

Detailed calculations, thresholds, and source identifiers.

Available for audit and advanced review.

Not part of the standard reading flow.

---

## Evidence Source Types

Evidence may include:

- deterministic Saju calculations
- psychological axis results
- survey answers
- relationship rule outputs
- interaction classifications
- repeated cross-source alignment
- contextual user input

Each source type must remain distinguishable internally.

The user-facing experience may simplify the language, but the system must preserve provenance.

---

## Evidence Tier

Every promoted insight should internally carry an evidence tier.

Recommended shared tiers:

### Corroborated

Multiple meaningful sources support the same direction.

### Refined

A primary signal is strengthened or clarified by another source.

### Tentative

The available signal is useful but incomplete or weakly supported.

Evidence tier affects:

- prominence
- wording
- certainty
- card eligibility
- recommendation strength

Evidence tier does not determine whether an insight is positive or negative.

---

## Confidence and Severity Are Separate

Confidence answers:

> How strongly can we support this interpretation?

Severity answers:

> How consequential may this pattern be?

A high-confidence minor difference is not a major risk.

A potentially serious issue with weak evidence must not be overstated.

The system must preserve both dimensions independently.

---

# 4.14 Layer 8 — Narrative System

## Purpose

Narrative converts structured findings into human understanding.

Narrative is not responsible for discovering unrestricted truths.

It is responsible for communicating eligible truths well.

---

## Deterministic Ownership

The deterministic layer owns:

- facts
- values
- direction
- classification
- thresholds
- eligibility
- evidence source
- evidence tier
- confidence
- severity
- suppression rules

## Narrative Ownership

The narrative layer owns:

- phrasing
- synthesis
- transitions
- examples grounded in approved findings
- emotional accessibility
- concise explanation
- relationship-centered framing
- language and locale adaptation

---

## Narrative Input Contract

The narrative layer should receive a structured package containing, where applicable:

- relationship type
- participant labels or names
- eligible findings
- source evidence
- direction
- confidence
- severity
- allowed interpretation range
- prohibited claims
- adjacent relevant findings
- required output length
- locale
- address and pronoun rules

The LLM should not reconstruct the analysis from raw data when a deterministic interpretation already exists.

---

## Narrative Output Contract

Narrative output should be limited to defined fields.

Examples:

- headline
- concise body
- recognition example
- transition
- reflection prompt
- practical implication

The LLM should not return:

- new scores
- new categories
- unsupported advice
- medical or clinical claims
- predictions
- hidden classifications
- contradictory direction
- fabricated user history

---

## Synthesis Requirement

Premium narrative should synthesize.

It should not merely rewrite evidence lines.

A meaningful synthesis usually connects at least two relevant findings.

Example:

Weak:

> One person has high structure. The other has high adaptability.

Stronger:

> One of you creates safety by planning ahead, while the other keeps the relationship flexible when plans change. This can make you effective together, but only when flexibility is not interpreted as carelessness and planning is not experienced as control.

The stronger version adds relationship meaning while preserving the original facts.

---

## Repetition Control

Before rendering, the system should compare:

- Hero
- Key Cards
- Graph captions
- Module openings
- Closing summary

Repeated conclusions should be:

- merged
- shortened
- referenced
- assigned a different function
- removed

A premium report should feel cumulative.

Not circular.

---

# 4.15 Layer 9 — Comparison Components

## Purpose

Comparison components help users understand difference without creating competition.

The goal is not to determine who is better.

The goal is to show how two patterns interact.

---

## Comparison Principles

Every comparison should communicate:

1. what each person tends to do
2. where the meaningful difference lies
3. how the difference affects the relationship
4. when the difference helps
5. when the difference creates friction

---

## Symmetry

Directional differences must be described symmetrically.

If the interpretation of A-high/B-low changes when the people are reversed, the reason must be contextual rather than biased wording.

The product must not implicitly assign:

- rational versus emotional
- strong versus weak
- mature versus immature
- active versus passive
- correct versus incorrect

unless a valid behavioral standard independently supports the distinction.

---

## Comparison Table Policy

Use a table when users need repeated side-by-side scanning.

Recommended columns:

| Situation | Person A tendency | Person B tendency | Relationship effect |
| --------- | ----------------- | ----------------- | ------------------- |

Avoid tables that contain long paragraphs.

When each cell becomes narrative-heavy, use paired cards or modules instead.

---

## Names and Labels

Use names when available and appropriate.

Avoid making users decode anonymous labels throughout the report.

Use Person A and Person B only when:

- names are unavailable
- privacy mode requires it
- technical reference demands it

Relationship-specific role language may be used when it improves clarity.

Examples:

- the person seeking resolution
- the person still processing
- the more structured partner
- the more socially energized person

Roles must describe the current pattern, not permanent identity.

---

# 4.16 Layer 10 — Reflection Components

## Purpose

Reflection connects system interpretation to lived experience.

Reflection should not feel like homework.

It should create a pause in the reading journey.

---

## Reflection Formats

Possible formats include:

- one-question pause
- private note
- shared conversation prompt
- real-life example recall
- agree / partly / not really response
- save for later
- compare perspectives

---

## Reflection Prompt Rules

A prompt should be:

- specific
- emotionally safe
- connected to the preceding insight
- answerable from experience
- non-accusatory

Good:

> After disagreements, who usually feels ready to reconnect first?

Weak:

> Do you communicate well?

Good:

> When social plans increase, does either person begin to feel overlooked or depleted?

Weak:

> Is one of you introverted?

---

## Reflection Data

If reflection responses are saved, they must be treated as a new evidence source.

They should not silently alter deterministic results.

The product should clearly distinguish:

- calculated interpretation
- user-confirmed experience
- user-disagreed experience
- future personalization

---

# 4.17 Layer 11 — Decision Support Components

## Purpose

Decision support organizes implications without replacing user judgment.

It helps users answer:

- What deserves attention?
- What is likely manageable?
- What may become costly if ignored?
- What should we discuss?
- What should we observe over time?

---

## Decision Support Structure

A decision-support section may include:

```text
What is working
What requires coordination
What may worsen under stress
What can be tested in real life
What remains uncertain
```

This is more useful than a binary verdict.

---

## Severity Ladder

The shared experience should use a consistent severity ladder.

### S0 — Strength

A resource the relationship can rely on.

### S1 — Difference

A meaningful variation that is not inherently harmful.

### S2 — Friction

A recurring mismatch that requires coordination.

### S3 — Strain

A pattern likely to create ongoing emotional or practical cost.

### S4 — Structural Risk

A pattern that may meaningfully threaten relationship stability or safety when supported by strong evidence.

Severity must be assigned deterministically.

The narrative layer may adjust tone but must not escalate or reduce severity.

---

## Structural Risk Restrictions

S4 must be rare.

It should require:

- a clearly defined risk construct
- strong evidence
- repeated or convergent signals
- appropriate contextual safeguards
- carefully limited language

The product must not imply abuse, danger, diagnosis, or inevitable failure based solely on personality or Saju data.

Where safety concerns arise from direct user disclosure, the product requires a separate safety-aware experience outside ordinary relationship scoring.

---

# 4.18 Layer 12 — Action Components

## Purpose

Action translates understanding into a realistic next step.

The report should not end with generic encouragement.

It should offer a small number of specific, proportionate actions.

---

## Action Types

Actions may include:

- conversation prompts
- expectation-setting
- role clarification
- schedule coordination
- reassurance preferences
- conflict pause agreements
- recovery rituals
- social-boundary planning
- decision protocols
- appreciation practices
- observation experiments

---

## Action Design Rules

Actions should be:

- grounded in the preceding analysis
- small enough to try
- behaviorally clear
- non-prescriptive
- appropriate to severity
- appropriate to confidence
- sensitive to relationship context

Good:

> Before a socially busy weekend, agree on which events are shared and which are optional.

Weak:

> Communicate more.

Good:

> When one person is ready to resolve a conflict earlier, use a specific time to reconnect rather than demanding immediate closure or leaving the return undefined.

Weak:

> Respect each other's differences.

---

## Action Quantity

Prefer:

- one primary action
- one optional experiment
- one conversation prompt

Do not overwhelm users with a long improvement checklist.

The product should increase agency, not create another performance burden.

---

# 4.19 Component Relationships

Components should create progressive depth.

Example:

```text
Hero
Different rhythms, strong mutual grounding.

↓

Key Card
You recover from emotional intensity at different speeds.

↓

Graph
Resilience and conflict-style comparison.

↓

Module
How conflict activation and recovery unfold.

↓

Evidence
Direction, source alignment, confidence.

↓

Reflection
Who tends to seek reconnection first?

↓

Action
Agree on a clear return time after taking space.
```

Each component contributes a different layer.

No component is redundant.

---

# 4.20 Summary Experience Versus Deep Experience

## Summary Experience

The summary should include only:

- identity and context
- Hero interpretation
- 2–3 key cards
- limited relationship measurement
- one high-value visual
- clear path into deeper modules

Target outcome:

> I understand the basic shape of this relationship.

## Deep Experience

The deep report should add:

- complete core dynamic modules
- meaningful comparisons
- supporting graphs
- layered evidence
- reflection
- decision support
- tailored actions
- methodology access

Target outcome:

> I understand why this relationship works the way it does and what deserves attention.

Deep does not mean repetitive.

Deep means causal, contextual, and useful.

---

# 4.21 Mobile and Desktop Behavior

The meaning hierarchy must remain consistent across devices.

## Mobile

Mobile should prioritize:

- one insight at a time
- vertically stacked cards
- simplified graphs
- expandable evidence
- short module openings
- persistent reading progress when useful

## Desktop

Desktop may support:

- paired comparisons
- wider evidence layouts
- side-by-side graph and interpretation
- anchored report navigation
- visible module overview

Desktop must not become a dense dashboard merely because space is available.

Whitespace remains part of the reading experience.

# 4.22 Accessibility

All relationship components must remain understandable without relying solely on:

- color
- shape
- animation
- hover
- visual proximity

Requirements include:

- text labels for scales
- readable contrast
- keyboard-accessible disclosures
- screen-reader descriptions for charts
- non-judgmental language
- clear score direction
- motion reduction support
- accessible mobile chart alternatives

A chart should have an equivalent textual takeaway.

# 4.23 Localization and Cultural Adaptation

Localization is not direct translation.

Relationship meaning changes across cultural contexts.

The shared structure remains stable, but examples and assumptions may adapt.

Areas requiring cultural care include:

- independence
- family involvement
- social obligations
- gender expectations
- household roles
- marriage expectations
- workplace hierarchy
- emotional directness
- public versus private affection
- financial coordination

The product must not assume that one cultural pattern is universally healthy.

However, cultural sensitivity must not erase genuine strain.

The system should distinguish:

- difference
- preference
- cultural norm
- coordination burden
- harmful behavior

# 4.24 Visual Language

The visual system should communicate:

- a medical dashboard

- an enterprise analytics tool

## Color

Color may distinguish:

Avoid simplistic green-good/red-bad treatment for multidimensional relationship patterns.

---

## Motion

Motion may support:

Motion must not:

- dramatize scores

- delay access to content

# 4.25 Content Suppression Rules

The system should suppress a component when:

- evidence is insufficient
- the finding duplicates a higher-priority insight
- the difference is too small to matter
- the user question is unrelated
- the interpretation is not stable
- the visual cannot be explained clearly
- the content would create false precision
- the component exists only to fill layout

Absence is preferable to filler.

A shorter accurate report is more premium than a longer generic one.

# 4.26 Cross-Product Consistency

- accessibility standards
- progressive disclosure

A user moving between relationship products should learn the system once—while still recognizing that each product answers a different life context.

# 4.29 Migration Principle

The current product should be migrated through controlled consolidation.

Recommended sequence:

```text
1. Inventory existing output
2. Identify canonical insights
3. Detect duplicated classifications
4. Assign each insight a component owner
5. Preserve valid deterministic logic
6. Remove conflicting client interpretation
7. Normalize confidence and evidence
8. Rebuild summary hierarchy
9. Reconnect deep modules
10. Validate with real local output
```

The migration should not begin by redesigning every screen.

It should begin by establishing truth ownership.

Visual redesign without logic consolidation will reproduce the same inconsistency in a more polished interface.

# 4.30 Local Product Validation

A component is not complete because:

- TypeScript passes
- a schema validates
- a snapshot test passes
- an LLM returns text
- the UI renders

The experience must also be reviewed as an actual report in the local product.

Validation should include:

- at least one similar pair
- at least one complementary pair
- at least one high-friction pair
- at least one mixed-confidence pair
- at least one limited-data pair
- Korean output
- English output
- mobile viewport
- desktop viewport

## Local Review Questions

Reviewers should ask:

### Immediate comprehension

- Can the relationship be understood within 10 seconds?
- Does the Hero describe the relationship rather than the people separately?
- Are the most important insights visible first?

### Card quality

- Are the cards distinct?
- Are they genuinely the strongest signals?
- Is any card generic?
- Is a weak card present only to fill the third slot?

### Measurement quality

- Does every score have a defined meaning?
- Is any number presented with false precision?
- Is confidence confused with relationship quality?
- Would a category communicate more honestly than a number?

### Graph quality

- Does each graph reveal something difficult to understand in prose?
- Are 11 axes being shown merely because they exist?
- Is the user's attention directed to the meaningful pattern?
- Can the graph be understood without reading a methodology document?

### Narrative quality

- Does the narrative synthesize rather than paraphrase?
- Does it repeat cards or graph captions?
- Does it preserve direction, confidence, and severity?
- Does it sound human without inventing lived history?

### Journey quality

- Does the report progress from recognition to understanding?
- Is there a moment of discovery?
- Is reflection connected to real experience?
- Does decision support avoid making the decision?
- Are actions specific and proportionate?

### Visual quality

- Does the result feel like one premium product?
- Is the page calm and readable?
- Are technical details visually subordinate?
- Is any component competing for attention without earning it?

# 4.31 Definition of Done

The shared Experience Component System is considered successfully implemented when:

1. every visible component has one documented responsibility
2. every major insight has a canonical deterministic owner
3. key cards are selected by evidence and user value
4. the system does not fabricate a third card
5. summary and deep reports serve different purposes
6. visible scores have defined constructs and methodology
7. unknown states are not converted into neutral-looking values
8. the 11 axes support relationship questions rather than dictate navigation
9. graphs are selected by task rather than visual preference
10. LLM narrative cannot create or change classifications
11. repeated insights are consolidated
12. social rhythm and energy differences are handled as meaningful relationship dynamics when relevant
13. confidence and severity remain separate
14. Korean and English outputs preserve the same canonical findings
15. mobile and desktop maintain the same information hierarchy
16. real local reports pass structured editorial and product review
17. the final output is credible enough to present to internal leadership as an intentional product, not a collection of features

---

# 4.32 Final Standard

The success of the component system is not measured by how many features appear on the page.

It is measured by whether users can move naturally from:

```text
What is happening between us?

↓

Why does it happen?

↓

Where does it show up?

↓

How much does it matter?

↓

What can we do with this understanding?
```

When every card, number, graph, paragraph, and interaction supports that movement, the report becomes a product rather than an output.
